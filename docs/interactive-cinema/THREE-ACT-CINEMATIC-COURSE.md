# OSA — Beyond the Model: three-act interactive course

## Series promise

A coherent, source-led journey from understanding AI, to understanding information power, to taking deliberate action. The audience should leave with better questions and practical verification habits—not fear, hype, or a promise that AI guarantees success.

## Act I — The machine that predicts

**Dramatic question:** If a system can produce a persuasive answer, what does that tell us—and what does it not tell us?

**Opening beat:** Ask the learner to solve a short classification or explanation task before revealing a model's answer. Compare the outputs, then ask which parts can be checked.

**Core scenes:**
1. The challenge — learner commits to an answer.
2. The model response — compare clarity, omissions, and confidence.
3. The mechanism — explain training, patterns, tokens, and inference through a map/forecast analogy; avoid claiming that the model reasons exactly like a person.
4. The failure — demonstrate a plausible but unsupported answer and require verification.
5. The turn — capability is task-specific; fluent language is not proof of truth.

**Interactive state:** learner chooses `trust`, `verify`, or `reject`. The next scene explains the consequence and provides a source-check workflow.

**Takeaway:** treat AI output as a useful hypothesis, not an authority.

## Act II — Who controls the knowledge pipeline?

**Dramatic question:** When information is abundant, who shapes what becomes visible, trusted, and actionable?

**Opening beat:** present three equally plausible headlines with different source quality and ask which should influence a high-stakes decision.

**Core scenes:**
1. Access — libraries, search, and public knowledge as distribution systems.
2. Infrastructure — data, compute, models, distribution, and institutional incentives.
3. Ranking — how recommendation and retrieval can affect visibility without proving truth.
4. The fork — choose convenience, transparency, or resilience for a fictional public information service.
5. The audit — compare trade-offs: access, privacy, pluralism, reliability, and accountability.

**Interactive state:** choices change the subsequent trade-off analysis, not the underlying factual record. Each route displays costs and unanswered questions.

**Takeaway:** knowledge power includes the ability to create information and the ability to control its access, ranking, and interpretation.

## Act III — Human agency in the AI era

**Dramatic question:** How can a person use AI to extend their capability without surrendering judgment?

**Opening beat:** a fictional team has one hour to research a new topic, produce a draft, and make a decision. The learner selects a workflow.

**Core scenes:**
1. Delegate — decide what can safely be handed to a model.
2. Verify — check sources, claims, calculations, and uncertainty.
3. Create — use AI to generate options, then apply human taste and context.
4. Decide — identify who is accountable for the outcome.
5. Commit — build a seven-day personal experiment with a measurable result.

**Interactive state:** learner chooses `speed-first`, `verification-first`, or `human-led`. The ending returns a tailored checklist and explains the trade-offs; no route guarantees success.

**Takeaway:** the durable skill is not merely prompt writing—it is framing the task, checking evidence, integrating context, and owning the decision.

## Series continuity

- Repeated motif: a thin line connecting a question, an evidence source, and a decision.
- Act I color accent: electric blue, representing capability and uncertainty.
- Act II accent: restrained crimson, representing influence and consequential choices.
- Act III accent: ice white and blue, representing clarity and agency.
- Each act opens with a concrete decision and ends with an unresolved question that leads to the next act.
- Motion should be purposeful; respect `prefers-reduced-motion` and provide keyboard-operable controls.

## Evidence rules

- Separate sourced facts, interpretation, and forecast in the interface.
- Prefer primary documentation, research papers, and official datasets.
- Date quantitative claims and show a direct source link.
- Never fabricate learner scores, usage metrics, endorsements, or case studies.
- Treat generated visuals as illustrative, not documentary footage.
- The content team must re-check sources before each major release.

## Learning checks

At the end of each act, ask three questions: one comprehension question, one evidence-evaluation question, and one transfer question. Explain why the answer is correct, rather than only showing a score.
